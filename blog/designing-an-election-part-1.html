Designing an election, Part 1: the constraints, and the system we have

India's general election as a system design problem. This part sets the rules, the assumptions, and describes the current design.

Prabhat Saxena · 26 October 2026 · 15 min read

What this is: a system-design model. Every fact about the current system is stated as an assumption with a plausible range, not as a verified claim. The argument is always "if these assumptions hold, this design trades X for Y", so no conclusion depends on a number being exact, current or complete.

What this is not: a statement of how elections should work, a fact-check of any procedure, or a position on any political, legal or partisan dispute.

Why model an election at all

Take away the politics and a general election is one of the largest coordinated engineering and logistics systems anywhere. It has a hard deadline and no retries. It has adversaries. And its most important user, the person who loses, has every reason to doubt the output.

That last point is what makes it unusual as a design problem. Most systems are built to be correct. An election has to be correct and visibly correct to people who would prefer it were not.

Most writing about elections is about who wins. This series is about how the machine works, and whether each part of it could work differently. In system design you do not improve a component by guessing. You write down what must never break, what is merely expensive, and then compare options against both. That is what this series does, one layer of the election at a time:

  1. Part 1 (this post): the constraints, the assumptions, and the current system and its flow.
  2. Parts 2 to 7: one layer each. For each layer I list approaches that could improve it, say what each one buys, and say which constraints each one puts under pressure.
  3. Part 8: putting the best choices together, and what engineering cannot settle.

One rule runs through the series, and it is the main idea of this post:

Some constraints are red lines: the things that make an election democratic at all. An approach is allowed to cross a red line, because pretending otherwise would hide the real cost. But when it does, the crossing is named in that approach's trade-offs and never buried.

Ground rules: a model, not a fact sheet

This series treats the election as a system design exercise, the way you would model a payment network or a content platform you do not run. That changes how to read everything that follows.

  1. Everything about the current system is an assumption, labelled A1, A2 and so on. The values are roughly what has been reported publicly, but I do not claim they are exact, current or complete, and no conclusion needs them to be.
  2. Every assumption has a plausible range. Conclusions are tested across that range. Where a conclusion flips inside the range, the post says so.
  3. Conclusions are conditional. "If A3 and A5 hold, then approach X buys Y and costs Z." A reader who knows a better number can change the assumption and re-read the conclusion.
  4. This is description and modelling, not advocacy. The question is "what does this design trade off?", never "what should India do?". Nothing here claims that any procedure is flawed, or secure, or should change.
  5. No claims about real incidents, parties or outcomes. Examples are generic.

1. The hard constraints: preserving democracy

These are not optimisation targets. If one is broken, what is left may be efficient, but it is no longer a democratic election. I have written each one as something you could, in principle, test.

IDHard constraintIn plain termsHow you would test it
H1Universal and equal suffrageEvery eligible adult can vote, and every vote carries the same weightCan any eligible voter be shut out by the process (not by law)? Does any voter get more than one vote?
H2Secrecy of the ballotNobody, including officials, can link a vote to a voter, and a voter cannot prove how they votedCan anyone, with any amount of access, reconstruct who voted for whom? Can a voter produce a receipt a buyer or coercer would accept?
H3Integrity of the countEvery valid vote is counted once, as cast; no vote is added, changed or droppedDoes the published count equal the set of votes cast?
H4VerifiabilityThe result can be checked by people the authority does not control, including losing candidatesCan an opposed party verify each critical step without taking the authority's word?
H5Independence of the authorityThe body that runs the election is not controlled by those who compete in itCan the government of the day instruct the body, or remove its head easily?
H6Free and fair competitionCandidates compete on a level field, and voters choose without coercion or briberyIs there an enforceable rule, and does it bind equally from announcement to result?
H7Finality and timelinessA legitimate result exists before the old house's term ends, and disputes have a defined pathIs there always a government that can be formed on time, and a defined way to challenge the result?
H8LegitimacyParticipants, including those who lose, accept the outcomeAn outcome of H1 to H7 made visible to people who have every reason to doubt it

Their assumed basis is A11: the independence of the authority, the secrecy of the ballot and the fixed term are taken as given, and the rest follow from what "free and fair" means.

Two observations that shape everything that follows:

  • H8 is the hardest, and it is not purely technical. A system can satisfy H1 to H7 and still fail H8 if people cannot see that it did. That is why verifiability (H4) matters as much as correctness (H3).
  • The red lines pull against each other. Perfect secrecy (H2) and perfect verifiability (H4) conflict: the easier it is to prove how you voted, the easier it is to coerce or buy a vote. Every design in this series sits somewhere on that line.

5. The current system, layer by layer

Seen as a design, a general election is a single, deadline-bound batch job, sharded by constituency, with a long write phase (voting) and a short, synchronised read phase (counting). It has nine layers; the first and third are mostly rule-bound, so the series concentrates on the others.

#LayerWhat it doesThe key controls (as assumed)The question for later parts
L0AuthorityRuns the whole processAn independent body, state-level chief electoral officers, district officers (A11)Not an engineering layer; it is what H5 protects
L1RegistryDecides who may vote, and whereElectoral rolls built through house visits by local officers, public claims and objections, voluntary ID linkingHow accurate, how fresh, how fair are the rolls?
L2Stations and logisticsPlaces voters, stations, staff and machinesThe station-size and distance planning rules (A5); minimum facilitiesQueues, travel, and people who cannot reach a station
L3Candidates and ballotsFixes who is on the ballot, and in what orderNominations, scrutiny, withdrawals, a final candidate list before ballots are loadedMostly rule-bound; few engineering choices
L4Machines and custodyPrepares, allocates and guards the voting machinesPre-election checks with party representatives, two-stage random allocation, sealing, strong rooms with CCTV and agentsHow strong is the chain of custody, and how visible is it?
L5Polling dayCaptures the voteIdentity check, indelible ink, a Control Unit that releases one ballot at a time, a Ballot Unit and a paper-trail printer, a mock poll first (A7)Speed, identity, secrecy, and what a voter can verify
L6Scheduling and securityDecides when each place votesPhases, central forces moved between them, silence periods and exit-poll bans (A6, A10)How many phases, in what order, for what cost
L7Counting and declarationTurns votes into resultsOne synchronised counting day, round-by-round tallies with agents, a count of paper slips from a sample of stations (A8), the Returning Officer's certificateAudit design, speed, and data transparency
L8DisputesHandles challengesElection petitions to a court within a time limit; machine components kept sealed for a period after the resultHow checkable is the evidence when it matters?

6. The end-to-end flow

One station, one day

  1. Before the polls open: a mock poll with party agents present; the machine is cleared and sealed.
  2. A voter arrives: one officer checks them against the roll.
  3. Marking: a second officer applies indelible ink and records the voter.
  4. Release: the officer at the Control Unit releases one ballot. The Ballot Unit will not accept a second vote until released again.
  5. Voting: the voter presses the button next to their choice, in a screened compartment.
  6. Confirmation: the printer shows a slip the voter can see for a few seconds before it drops into a sealed box.
  7. At the close of the poll: the Control Unit is closed, the tally is recorded in a form, copies go to party agents, and everything is sealed with agents' signatures.
  8. Custody: the machines travel under guard to a strong room and stay there until counting.

Each step exists because of a red line: the roll check serves H1, the one-ballot-at-a-time release serves H3, the screened compartment serves H2, the printed slip and the agent's copy serve H4, and the signatures serve H8.

A pattern worth noticing

Two design habits repeat through the flow, and they will recur in every later part:

  • Distributed observation. Instead of one authority asserting correctness, opposed parties each have their own agents at each critical step. It works like consensus among mutually distrusting parties: no single actor is trusted, because every step is witnessed by actors with opposed interests.
  • A small blast radius. The unit of work is one station, so almost every failure can be repaired locally (replace a machine, re-run one station) without redoing the election.

Both buy robustness and legitimacy, and both cost people and time. That cost is where the later parts look for room to improve.

7. Where the friction is (questions, not conclusions)

These are the places later parts will examine. I list them as questions because I do not yet know how much each one costs, and I would rather model than assume.

  1. Roll accuracy and upkeep. How are deaths, moves and duplicates found, and what does each wrong entry cost?
  2. Migrants and distance. What does the "vote where you are registered" rule cost in turnout?
  3. Queue time and station load. Where does waiting arise, and where does the load concentrate?
  4. Custody visibility. How can an opposed party check the chain of custody without standing guard for weeks?
  5. Audit design. How much does a fixed sample of paper slips tell us, and what else could it be?
  6. Phase count. What does each extra phase cost in time, money and exposure, and what does each fewer phase demand?
  7. Counting-day peak and data. How quickly can results be produced and published in a form others can check?
  8. Dispute evidence. When a result is challenged, how good is the evidence that survives?

8. What this post is and is not

It is an engineer's model of a system, with its inputs listed as assumptions (A1 to A11) and ranges. It describes the current design in simplified form, to have something concrete to analyse. It is not a description of how any procedure is actually carried out on the ground, not a verification of any claim, and not a view on how things should be. I am not an election official or a lawyer, and I have left out campaign finance, party politics, delimitation and outcomes on purpose.

Where the assumptions were drawn from

For the curious, and not needed to follow the argument: the Election Commission of India's public press notes and 2024 general election pages, the Commission's published elector brochures, and press reports of the 2024 schedule, the turnout statement and the April 2024 Supreme Court ruling on paper-trail verification. They are the source of the assumptions, not claims this series stands behind.
