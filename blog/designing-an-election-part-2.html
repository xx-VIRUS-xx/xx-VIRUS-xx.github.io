Designing an election, Part 2: the registry

How does the electoral roll stay true without excluding anyone? Six ways to improve it, scored on the same constraints as everything else in the series.

Prabhat Saxena · 27 October 2026 · 21 min read

This is Part 2 of a series. Part 1 sets the red lines (H1 to H8) and the assumptions (A1 to A11) used here.

The framing: cleaning a roll is a classification problem

Part 1 said the registry answers one question: who may vote, and where. Keeping that list true is a classification problem. Each entry is either a real, eligible, unique person at the right address, or it is not. Any process that cleans the roll is a classifier, whether it is a clerk with a form or a matching algorithm.

Like every classifier, it makes two kinds of error, and they do not cost the same.

ErrorWhat it isWhat it costs
False removalA real voter is flagged or deletedThe person is shut out of the process. This is a direct H1 failure, and it lands on a person, not a statistic
False retentionA duplicate, a dead person or a person who has moved stays on the rollRoll noise. It is a latent risk to H1 (equal weight) and H8 (does the roll look credible?), and it needs another act, such as someone casting the extra vote, before it becomes harm

Two things follow, and the rest of the post builds on them.

  1. The costs are asymmetric. A false removal is visible, personal and hard to reverse on polling day. A false retention is diffuse and mostly invisible until someone looks. A design that tunes only for the second will produce the first.
  2. The threshold is a policy choice wearing an engineering costume. Wherever a system says "flag at or above this score", someone has chosen how many real voters they are willing to put through a notice process to catch one duplicate. That choice should be explicit, not buried in a parameter.

Because of the asymmetry, I attach one safeguard to every approach in this post:

Due process, stated once and repeated for each option: no deletion without notice and a chance to object. An approach may find candidates for deletion by any method. It may not remove a name until the person has been told and had a defined way to respond.

1. Current flow and assumed baseline

In simplified form, the roll today is a locally maintained register. Officers visit households, a draft is published, anyone may file claims and objections in a window, and a final roll is published. Between revisions, individuals can apply to add, move, correct or delete an entry, and voluntary linking of an ID number is encouraged. Part 1's L1 row summarises this.

I model it with these new assumptions. All of them are replaceable with data.

3. Approaches

Part 2 outline items A1 to A6 are renamed R1 to R6 here to avoid clashing with the assumption IDs. I kept all six and did not merge any. R3 builds on R2 and R4 can be added to any of them, but each is worth scoring alone.

R1. Event-driven updates from civil records

Idea. A death, a birth or a change of address recorded elsewhere in government triggers a proposed update to the roll, instead of waiting for a house visit.

How it works. A feed from the civil registry reaches the roll's matching component. A match produces a proposal (remove this entry, add this person at 18, move this address). A local officer confirms, and a notice goes to the person or the household before any deletion.

Advantages. Fresher rolls, less house-visit labour, and a continuous process in place of a periodic one.

Trade-offs.

  • Feed quality. Under A2.4 the feed is about 80% complete. The 20% it misses is not random: it is likely to be people at the margins of the civil system. If the roll relies on the feed, those people stay stale, and their absence is invisible. That is an H1 pressure (~).
  • Wrong matches. A death record matched to the wrong living person is a false removal triggered by a data-entry error. This is the single scariest failure of the approach and the reason for the notice step.
  • Purpose limitation and privacy. Data collected for one purpose (recording a death for inheritance, say) is reused for another. The question is whether the law permits it, whether the person was told, and who can see the linked data. Contested positions exist on both sides: one says it is a modest, efficient reuse with a narrow output; the other says each new link widens the surveillance surface regardless of intent. Under this model the cost shows up as H1 ~ (people avoid civil registration if it has side effects) and H8 ~ (trust).
  • Due process interaction. Compatible. Events create proposals, not deletions. The cost is that notice and response add weeks, so a stale roll lasts a little longer to stay safe.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R1 Event-driven updates~✓✓~✓✓✓~-++

What would make it acceptable. A documented legal basis for the data flow; a named owner for each feed; a measured match precision; deletion only after notice; and an audit that estimates who the feed misses.

R2. ID-linked deduplication

Idea. Use a national ID number to find the same person appearing twice.

How it works. Entries with the same ID number are compared. Exact matches are strong evidence of a duplicate, much stronger than names and ages.

Advantages. Very high precision where the ID is present. A cheap, explainable rule.

Trade-offs.

  • Voluntary linking (H1 ~). Under A2.5 about 70% are linked, so the approach only sees the linked 70%. It is strong on that part and silent on the rest. Duplicates among unlinked entries remain.
  • Mandatory linking (H1 ✗). If the rule becomes "no linked ID, no entry", then the roughly 30% without a link (A2.5) are at risk of removal unless they link. People without an ID, with a mismatched name, or with a transcription error would be removed through no fault of their own. This crosses the H1 red line directly.
  • What would make mandatory linking acceptable. Universal ID coverage; a free, fast, accessible correction path for mismatches; a rule that a failed link triggers outreach, never deletion; and measured data on how many mismatches exist. Even then, the cost is a large one-off correction effort and a standing risk that errors in the ID database leak into the roll.
  • Privacy and purpose limitation. A roll that holds an ID number becomes a join key to everything else. That is the core of the contested position: the same linkage that catches duplicates also lets other datasets be joined to the roll. Mitigation: keep the ID out of the published roll and use a one-way hashed token for matching inside a restricted environment.
  • Due process interaction. Needs to be explicit. A match is a reason to open a case, not to close one.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R2a ID linking, voluntary~✓✓✓✓✓✓~-+0
R2b ID linking, mandatory✗✓✓✓✓✓✓~-+-

R3. Probabilistic deduplication with a human review queue

Idea. Score pairs of entries for how likely they are the same person, using name, age, relative's name, address and photograph, then send the highest-scoring pairs to a reviewer.

How it works. Blocking limits comparisons to plausible neighbours. A score is computed per pair. A threshold decides which pairs are flagged. A reviewer examines each flag, and a notice goes out before any entry is deleted.

Advantages. Works without an ID. Tunable: lower the threshold to catch more, raise it to protect more. Scales through the review queue.

Trade-offs. This is where the classification framing becomes concrete. The next section runs the numbers.

  • False positives are real voters. Under A2.3, duplicates are rare, so even a decent classifier produces many false alarms (see the table).
  • Notice and hearing. Every false positive costs someone a notice, possibly a hearing, and an anxious interval. Voters who do not respond (migrants, the elderly, those who do not read) risk deletion by silence, which is a quiet H1 breach. Mitigation: a non-response never triggers deletion alone; an unconfirmed case triggers a visit.
  • Reviewer workload. Set by the threshold, not by the number of voters. A policy decision about review capacity is a decision about the threshold.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R3 Probabilistic dedupe + review~✓✓~✓✓✓~-+-

What would make it acceptable. A published threshold and its measured false-positive rate; a rule that no deletion rests on a score alone; a visit before any silence-based removal; and an independent sample audit of both removed entries and retained ones.

R4. A public, diff-able roll with a change log

Idea. Publish every addition, deletion and correction as a dated, append-only log, so anyone can reconstruct the roll at any date and see what changed.

How it works. The roll is a versioned dataset. Each change records what, when, under which rule and by which office. Outsiders can diff two dates or sample entries.

Advantages. The biggest verifiability gain in this part. A mass deletion would be visible as a spike in the log. A party, a researcher or a citizen can run their own checks, so no one has to take the authority's word (H4 +, H8 +). It also supports due process: a wrongly deleted person can show when and why.

Trade-offs.

  • Privacy and harassment. A public file with names, relatives and addresses can be abused for targeting, harassment or doxxing, and for guessing a household's composition. For some groups (a woman living alone, a person who has fled a threat) that is a safety risk, not an inconvenience.
  • H2 (indirect). The roll holds no vote, so secrecy of the ballot is not directly affected. But a public map of who lives where, joined with turnout lists, can inform coercion. I score it ~.
  • H4 and H8 versus safety. Contested. One position holds that maximal public disclosure is the only real check; the other holds that a roll searchable only by the person concerned, with aggregate statistics public, protects people at little verifiability cost. Scored the same way, the middle options (pseudonymised public log, full detail to accredited auditors, aggregates to everyone) keep most of the H4 gain with a smaller privacy cost.
  • Due process interaction. Strongly positive: the log is the evidence trail for any notice.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R4a Fully public log with identities✓~✓✓✓~✓✓-0-
R4b Pseudonymised log + aggregates + accredited auditors✓✓✓✓✓✓✓✓-0-

The H6 ~ in R4a is about intimidation of voters at the roll level, not about competition between parties. R4b trades some outsider reach (only accredited parties can verify an individual entry) for safety.

What would make R4a acceptable. A strong harassment-response channel; a protected-entry category for people at risk; and a limit on bulk download paired with abuse detection. These all cost operations and money.

R5. Self-service registration and status tracking

Idea. A person registers, checks their status, tracks an application and files a correction without queueing at an office.

How it works. A web or phone service, plus assisted kiosks. A status number tracks each application through its stages. Every request is logged, and the result is communicated back.

Advantages. Lower per-case cost, less dependence on an intermediary, and a visible trail that answers "where is my application?". It also gives people a way to discover that they were removed, before polling day.

Trade-offs.

  • Digital divide (H1 ~). Under A2.8 about 40% could not do it unaided. If self-service replaces the paper path rather than adding to it, those people are worse off. The mitigation is non-negotiable: keep an assisted path (local officers, community centres) for as long as A2.8 is under about 90%.
  • Fraud and false applications. An easy online form also makes mass false applications easier, so identity checks at submission matter. That raises the exclusion risk for the 40%. Both effects are real.
  • Due process interaction. Positive when the status check also shows pending notices, and negative when a notice is only delivered online.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R5 Self-service + status tracking~✓✓✓✓✓✓✓+++

What would make it acceptable. The assisted path stays funded and visible; notices go out by more than one channel; and take-up is reported by region and by group, so a gap shows up.

R6. Automatic enrolment at 18

Idea. A person who turns 18 and is already in a civil record is added to the roll without applying.

How it works. The age and residence fields from a civil or education record generate a proposed entry on the 18th birthday. The person receives a notice and can correct or decline.

Advantages. Directly attacks the gap in A2.7 (H1 +). The enrolment decision no longer depends on the young person knowing the process.

Trade-offs.

  • Consent and data sharing. Registering a person without an application changes the legal posture from opt-in to opt-out. Contested: supporters call it the removal of a barrier; critics call it the state acting on a person's behalf without being asked. Both positions score the same on H1 and differ on whether consent is a constraint of the model. I treat it as a design parameter that must be named. I do not rank it.
  • Wrong place. Auto-enrolment at a parent's address may place a student or worker at the wrong constituency. That is an H1 problem of a quieter kind: enrolled, but unable to vote where they live. Mitigation: ask for the current address in the notice.
  • Feed completeness. Same as R1 (A2.4): people absent from the feed stay absent. The gap is narrowed, not closed.
  • Due process interaction. Adding is not deleting, so the safeguard applies only to the decline and correction path.

Scorecard.

H1H2H3H4H5H6H7H8CostTimeEffort
R6 Automatic enrolment at 18✓✓✓✓✓✓✓~-++

The H8 ~ is about whether the opt-out design is trusted, and it depends on how clearly it is explained.

4. A worked example: moving a dedupe threshold (R3)

This is a synthetic model, not data. The script is dedupe_tradeoff.py (stdlib only, no randomness: expected counts from normal tails).

Setup, all invented for illustration:

  • A roll of 1,000,000 entries.
  • After blocking, 500,000 candidate pairs of different people who look somewhat alike.
  • True-duplicate pairs have a similarity score centred at 0.80. Different-person pairs centre at 0.40.
  • Two ranges swept: the duplicate rate (0.5% and 3%, bracketing A2.3) and the score noise (sd 0.08 for clean data, sd 0.15 for noisy data such as spelling variants and missing fields).

What the tables say, and the point of the exercise:

  1. Data quality moves the whole curve. At threshold 0.70 with clean data, 44 real voters are wrongly flagged per million entries. With noisy data the same threshold wrongly flags 11,375. The threshold did not change. The data did.
  2. Where duplicates are rare, most flags are false alarms. At 0.5% duplicates and noisy data, a 0.70 threshold gives 75% of flags that are real voters. Precision is a property of the base rate, not only of the algorithm.
  3. There is no free threshold. In the noisy 0.5% case, going from 0.70 to 0.90 cuts wrongly flagged voters from 11,375 to 215, and leaves 3,738 duplicates in place instead of 1,262.
  4. The workload scales with the roll. Scaling linearly to the 97 crore roll of A1, a roughly 4,400-flag-per-million outcome means about 4.3 million cases. At 10 minutes each (A2.9) that is about 700,000 reviewer hours. At 30 minutes it is three times that. This is arithmetic from invented numbers, so read it as a shape: the review queue is the real cost of the approach.
  5. Review changes the cost of a false flag, not its existence. Suppose, again synthetically, that 5% of wrongly flagged real voters are wrongly removed anyway (they do not respond and the follow-up fails). In the noisy 0.5% case at 0.80, that is about 96 real voters per million entries, roughly 93,000 across a 97 crore roll. At 0.90 the figure drops to about 11 per million. The number to watch is the residual after review, not the raw flag count.

The model also shows the reverse: stopping at a high threshold to protect voters leaves most duplicates in place (22,425 of 30,000 in the 3% noisy case at 0.90). Neither end of the dial is safe, and the only way to move the whole curve is better data, which is what R1, R2 and R5 aim at.

No bundle removes all risk. Each is a stance, with something given up.

Contested points inside the bundles: whether to adopt R2b (the most effective dedupe, the sharpest red-line crossing), and whether R4a or R4b is the right level of disclosure. Under this model, R2b scores H1 ✗ and R4a scores H2 ~ and H6 ~, and the same scorecard applies to whichever position a reader holds.

7. What I would test first, and what I could not measure

Test first.

  1. Measure the false-positive rate on a real sample, before any threshold is set. The worked example shows the rate depends mostly on data quality. A manually verified sample of a few thousand flagged pairs gives that curve for real data, and it is the single cheapest piece of evidence for choosing between R3 thresholds.
  2. Measure the feed coverage of R1 and R6. Compare a sample of known deaths and 18th birthdays against the feed. This replaces A2.4 with data.
  3. Pilot R5 with a parallel assisted path in two or three contrasting regions and report take-up by group (A2.8).
  4. Test R4b on a closed group of accredited auditors before any public release, to see what they can and cannot verify.

What I could not measure.

  • Every number in the worked example is synthetic. The shapes are robust, the values are not.
  • The real stale rate, duplicate rate and ID-linking share (A2.2, A2.3, A2.5) are not known to me.
  • How many people avoid civil registration because of what it triggers. That behavioural cost of R1 and R2 is the hardest to see and may be the largest.
  • Whether published logs reduce or raise harassment. It cuts both ways, and I have no data on which dominates.

8. Limits of this write-up

This is a model with invented parameters, not a description of how any roll is built or checked. I have left out the legal framework for citizenship and residence, how overseas and service electors are registered, and the question of who is eligible, which is a legal matter and not an engineering one. I take no view on any current roll practice. The scorecards show how designs trade off against H1 to H8 under the stated assumptions, and a reader with better numbers can re-read them.
